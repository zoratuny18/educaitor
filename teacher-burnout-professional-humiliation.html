Teacher Burnout Isn't the Problem. Professional Humiliation Is.

The burned-out teacher down the hall is not a teacher who stopped caring. She was asked to carry more and more responsibility while being treated as if her professional judgment didn't matter.

The burned-out teacher down the hall is not a teacher who stopped caring. She is not a teacher who suddenly became less passionate or less committed.

The wrong diagnosis

The burned-out teacher used to be the teacher with the spark in her eyes. She gave everything she had to her classroom. A few years later, people call her "burned out." I don't think that's the right diagnosis. It's too simplistic. What happened wasn't simply exhaustion. It was years of being asked to carry more and more responsibility while being treated as if her professional judgment didn't matter. The work kept growing. Her influence kept shrinking. Eventually, the message became impossible to ignore:

"We trust you with everything except your opinion."

The problem isn't simply policy or administrative work. Planning lessons, differentiation, grading, communicating with parents, coordinating with paraprofessionals, faculty meetings, curriculum alignment, mandatory training, newsletters, emails—these are all legitimate parts of teaching. The thing is, nowadays this counts as 75% of teaching, plus 25% instruction. Yes, all of this is completely out of control. Newsletters could be removed from the list entirely, among other things, but the problem runs deeper. Teachers are required to do all of this work, invest their professional judgment into every decision, and then discover that their judgment carries almost no weight. They spend the most time with students, know them better than anyone else in the building, yet too often their voice is treated as optional rather than essential.

Responsibility without authority

That is what burns people out. It isn't merely the existence of administrative work; it is being expected to carry enormous responsibility without meaningful authority. Teachers are asked to think deeply, solve problems, and make impossible situations work, only to have decisions overridden by people further removed from the classroom. After pouring every ounce of expertise into a student or a class, the message they receive is simple: your opinion doesn't really matter.

Over time, that is profoundly demoralizing. It teaches them that their experience counts less than a meeting agenda, a policy document, or avoiding conflict.

How schools reinforce it

Most schools reinforce this dynamic. Meetings become exercises in compliance rather than collaboration. Teachers are expected to carefully navigate parent complaints, literally instructed to walk on eggshells. In some schools, administrators seem more afraid of upsetting students than of undermining their own team of professionals—the very people they actually hired to do the job. The result is a workplace where everyone performs the appearance of professionalism while the professionals are being reduced to the level of cheap workers.

The spark, consumed

First-year teachers often enter the profession believing they can change lives. They arrive with energy, optimism, and a genuine sense of mission. The system quickly learns who will say yes, who will stay late, who will absorb extra work, and who will sacrifice for students. In my experience, this is 95% of teachers, which makes them the easiest people to overload. The spark that brought them into teaching is slowly consumed. It's sad to watch.

The door is open

Luckily, life is happening, the field is changing, humans invent things, and progress is real. Teaching has been massively affected by COVID-19, mandating everyone—all teachers, globally—to navigate online learning by using technology. Everyone adapted. We can discuss the pros and cons of this, but that is not the topic. The point is this: the door to technology in education has been opened. Finally, tech is in.

Then came AI. Many were skeptical. A lot of people still are. But the psychological barriers are starting to wear off. The guards are coming down. We are an adaptive species. Now it is just a matter of time. It is a when, not an if, until all teachers will use AI in one way or another. I elaborate on this in "AI Is Coming for Teachers. Own Your Tools, or Be Owned."

This is good news because the technology available to us today is part of the solution. It is not another wellness webinar or another reminder about self-care. It is about restoring teachers as professionals. But expecting the system to do it is naïve, so teachers are encouraged to take things into their own hands.

Take it into your own hands

First, make sure you reduce the time spent on administrative work so you spend more of your time teaching and building relationships, because relationships—not paperwork—are the foundation of learning.

Second, make sure your professional judgment is loud and clear. If your voice isn't heard, leave. I'm not joking. There is a massive teacher shortage everywhere. Fear not. Leave, and encourage all your colleagues to leave as well, until the school system learns.

Find a school that backs you up—always—and treats you as a professional. If you have been told you would be treated this way and figured out mid-year that you weren't, leave mid-year.

You are needed

Keep in mind: you are needed. Without you, there is nothing—the system collapses. You are literally the single most important part of the education system, and you are treated the worst. You spend every day with students, which makes you the single person who possesses unique knowledge that no report, dashboard, or committee can replace.

Ignoring your expertise is not an unfortunate error; it is intentional poor decision-making.

Why? Because schools are more interested in pretending they provide "excellence" and "high-quality education" than in genuinely providing those things to students.

To administrators

To school administrators and directors who read this: if you want to keep great teachers, you must stop asking them to carry all the responsibility while denying them a meaningful voice in the decisions that affect their classrooms.
